<p>Corrected on 2026-11-02 after a question from Jibin: the earlier version of this section reported the best of two walks and so hid the cost of the first one. Method: a chain is built and walked once on one core, so every line of it sits in that core's chiplet (in its slices, and the last 2 MiB of the walk in its L2). A core on the same or the other chiplet then walks two fresh chains over the same lines, one after the other, and the time of each walk is kept. Pairs across chiplets: built on 3 and walked on 67, 67 to 3, 64 to 3, 3 to 119; same-chiplet controls: 3 to 3 and 67 to 67. At 16 MiB the same-chiplet walks take 29.0 to 29.3 ns per load. Across chiplets the first walk takes 85.0 to 88.5 ns per load, and the second walk 31.7 to 33.2 ns. So a line that the other chiplet's slices hold costs about 56 to 59 ns more on first access: the load misses in the proxy slice on the reader's own mesh, crosses to the slice on the other mesh that holds the line, and comes back. Once read, the line is kept in a slice on the reader's own mesh, and the second walk is close to the own-mesh value. The remaining 2.6 to 4 ns of the second walk above each core's own-mesh figure (28.9 to 29.4 ns for cores 3, 64, 67 and 119 [results/followup3-20261102T031936Z/percore-latency-1.csv]) is not explained. At 32 MiB: first walk 82.8 to 87.7 ns, second 32.7 to 37.3 ns, same-chiplet 30.2 ns. At 64 MiB DRAM misses enter: first walk 93.6 to 97.9 ns against 48.7 to 51.5 ns same-chiplet. The 85 to 88 ns first walk agrees with the 81 to 88 ns Bill reports for "L3, other domain", so the earlier hypothesis that his lines were dirty in a remote L2 is withdrawn. Caveat: about one eighth of the lines (the last 2 MiB walked by the builder) may still be in the builder's L2 at the first walk and be served core to core; the test does not separate them.
