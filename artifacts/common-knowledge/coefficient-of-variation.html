Coefficient of Variation

CV answers one question: how big is the wobble compared to the size of the thing?

CV = standard deviation (σ)mean (μ) × 100%

Standard deviation measures spread in the data's own units (microseconds, tokens per second, grams). Dividing by the mean removes the units, leaving a pure percentage. That makes CV good for one specific job: comparing spread between datasets whose typical values are different sizes.

3 · The trap: a constant offset shrinks CV without shrinking the wobble

Adding a fixed amount to every measurement moves the mean but leaves the standard deviation untouched — deviations from the mean don't change when everything shifts together. Since CV = σ/μ, the ratio drops even though nothing got steadier.

A real case from our own lab (per-token generation period, two experiment arms where the second arm deliberately added a constant ~2.44 ms wait to every token):

arm 1 (normal)5903 µs617 µs10.4%
arm 2 (normal + constant 2441 µs)8344 µs601 µs7.2%
Rule
When two datasets differ by a known constant offset (a fixed added delay, a baseline shift), compare their standard deviations in absolute units, or rescale both CVs to a common mean. In the example: 601/5903 ≈ 10.2% vs 10.4% — the honest comparison shows almost no change.

4 · When CV is the right tool, and when it is not

  • Right tool: comparing steadiness across different scales — response time of a 100 ms service vs a 1000 ms service, throughput of machines with different speeds, weights of animals of different sizes. CV is unitless, so it also compares quantities measured in different units.
  • Only for ratio scales. The data must have a true zero (time, length, count, rate). For scales with an arbitrary zero — temperature in °C, a score offset by a baseline — the mean is arbitrary, so σ/μ is meaningless.
  • Breaks near zero. If the mean is close to 0 (or the data crosses 0), CV blows up or flips sign and stops being interpretable.
  • Watch the constant-offset trap (section 3): any change that shifts the mean without touching the spread changes CV. Ask "did σ move, or only μ?" before celebrating a lower CV.
  • Rough benchmark-world calibration: for repeated performance runs, CV < 1% is usually considered tight, 1–5% moderate, > 5% noisy — but the threshold that matters is whatever effect size you are trying to measure: the effect should be several times the CV.
One-line summary
CV = standard deviation ÷ mean, in percent: the wobble as a fraction of the typical value. Use it to compare spread across different scales; do not use it when the zero is arbitrary, the mean is near zero, or the means differ by a constant you added yourself.
